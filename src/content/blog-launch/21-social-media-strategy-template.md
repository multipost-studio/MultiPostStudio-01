A social media strategy is a short written plan that says why you post, who you post for, what you post about, where you publish, how often, and how you will judge success. For a small team, one page is enough. If the plan doesn't fit on a page, nobody will follow it.

## Key takeaways

- A social media strategy only needs six decisions: goal, audience, content pillars, channels, cadence and measurement.
- Fewer channels done consistently usually beat many channels done sporadically, especially with a team of one to three people.
- A strategy is a working document. Put a monthly review on the calendar, or it becomes a file nobody opens.
- Write down what you will not do. Clear exclusions protect a small team's time more than any extra tactic.

## Why small teams need a strategy more than big ones

Big teams can absorb waste. They have a designer for the graphics, a copywriter for the captions and someone else to catch a missed week. A team of two does not have that slack, so every hour spent on a post that serves no purpose is an hour taken from something that does.

A written strategy is mostly a filter. When someone suggests starting a TikTok account, joining a trending format or replying to every comment within ten minutes, you check it against the page. Does it serve the goal? Does it fit a pillar? Can we sustain it at our cadence? If not, the answer is "not now", and you don't have to argue about it again.

## The one-page template

Copy these six headings into a document and answer each in two or three sentences. If you need more room, you are probably being vague somewhere else.

1. **Goal.** One primary outcome for the next quarter. For example: "Send more qualified visitors to the pricing page" or "Get 20 more people to book a demo call." Pick one, not four.
2. **Audience.** Who you are talking to, in one sentence, plus the problem they are trying to solve. "Owners of independent gyms who are tired of no-shows" beats "fitness people."
3. **Content pillars.** Three to five recurring themes that every post belongs to. A content pillar is a topic you commit to covering repeatedly, such as "customer questions answered" or "behind the scenes."
4. **Channels.** The two or three platforms where your audience actually spends time, and the role each one plays.
5. **Cadence.** How many posts per channel per week you can sustain for three months without strain.
6. **Measurement.** The two or three numbers you will check monthly, and what you will do if they move or stall.

## How to fill it in, step by step

The order matters. Each answer constrains the next, so resist the urge to start with channels.

### Start with the goal, then work backwards

Write the goal as a business outcome, then ask what social media can plausibly contribute. A local bakery might say "more weekend pre-orders". A B2B software team might say "more trial sign-ups from people who first met us on LinkedIn." Social media rarely produces sales alone, so name the specific action you want from a viewer: click, save, sign up, visit, reply.

### Describe one real audience

Look at your last ten customers, clients or newsletter subscribers. What did they ask before buying? Which words did they use? Those questions are your raw material for posts, and their vocabulary is your voice. If you can't answer, your first month of content is partly research: post, read the comments and replies, and note what people ask.

### Choose pillars you can produce on a normal week

A pillar that needs a film crew will collapse by week three. Choose themes you can produce with the tools and time you already have. A freelance designer might pick "before and after", "tool tips", "client questions" and "process notes." Each pillar should be able to generate at least a dozen post ideas, so you don't run dry.

### Pick channels by audience and by effort

For each channel, write one line: "LinkedIn: credibility with buyers, two posts a week." "Instagram: show the product in use, three posts a week." If a channel has no clear role, drop it for now. You can add it later when the first ones run smoothly.

Also note each platform's format rules, because they shape what you can say. As of 2026, X counts standard posts up to 280 characters, Bluesky allows 300 graphemes, and LinkedIn posts can run to 3,000 characters. A strategy that ignores those differences produces captions that need rewriting at the last minute.

### Set a cadence you can keep

Cadence is a promise to yourself, so make it modest. If your team can reliably make three good posts a week, write "three posts a week" and not "daily." You can raise it later. Missing a promised cadence erodes the habit faster than a low cadence ever would.

### Decide your measurement before you publish

Choose metrics that connect to the goal. If the goal is website visits, track link clicks. If it's awareness, track reach and follows. If it's conversation, track replies and saves. Ignore the rest until the first three feel routine. Add UTM tags (short labels appended to a link) so your analytics tool can show which posts sent visitors.

## Keep it alive: a monthly review

A strategy that isn't reviewed drifts. Put a 45-minute review on the calendar once a month and walk through four questions:

- Which three posts performed best against our goal, and what did they have in common?
- Which pillar produced the least, and should it be reshaped or retired?
- Did we hit our cadence? If not, what got in the way?
- What is one thing we will test next month?

Write the answers directly beneath the template with the date. After a few months, those notes become a record of what your audience actually responds to, and that is more valuable than any generic benchmark.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Goals that cannot be measured.** "Build brand awareness" is fine as a theme, but pair it with something you can check, such as reach or profile visits.
- **Copying a larger competitor.** Their cadence rests on a team and budget you don't have.
- **Treating every platform the same.** One caption pasted everywhere ignores each platform's limits and norms.
- **No exclusions.** Add a short "we will not" list: no daily posting, no chasing every trend, no channels without a named owner.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a social media strategy be?

One page is enough for a small team. If it covers goal, audience, pillars, channels, cadence and measurement in a few sentences each, it is complete. Longer documents tend to go unread.

### How often should I update my social media strategy?

Review it monthly and rewrite it quarterly. Monthly reviews adjust tactics, such as which pillar to emphasise. A quarterly rewrite revisits the goal itself and whether your channels still make sense.

### How many platforms should a small team use?

Start with two or three at most. Choose the platforms where your audience already is, and give each a defined role. Add another only when your existing channels run consistently without strain.

### What is the difference between a strategy and a content calendar?

A strategy explains why and what: goals, audience and themes. A content calendar is the schedule of specific posts and dates that carries out the strategy. You need the strategy first, or the calendar fills with random posts.
