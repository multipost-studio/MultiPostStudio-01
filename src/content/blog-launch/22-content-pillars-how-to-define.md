Content pillars are three to five recurring topics that every one of your social media posts belongs to. They give your feed a recognisable shape, make planning faster because you choose a pillar before you choose an idea, and help your audience understand what to expect from you.

## Key takeaways

- Content pillars are recurring themes, not formats: a pillar is a subject you cover, while a format is how you present it.
- Three to five pillars is a workable range for most small teams. More than that tends to blur your message and strain your production time.
- A good pillar sits where your audience's needs, your expertise and your business goal overlap.
- You can test a pillar by listing 12 post ideas under it. If you can't, it is too narrow.

## What a content pillar is (and what it isn't)

A pillar is a topic you commit to returning to. "Customer questions answered" is a pillar. "Reels" is not, because it names a format rather than a subject. The same pillar can appear as a carousel on Instagram, a text post on LinkedIn and a short video on TikTok.

This distinction matters because it keeps your planning flexible. When you plan by pillar, you decide the message first and adapt it to each platform afterwards. When you plan by format, you often end up making content because the format is trending, not because it helps anyone.

## How to find your pillars

Three inputs produce good pillars. Write down what you find for each, then look for the overlaps.

1. **What your audience needs.** Review the last 20 questions customers asked by email, in comments or on calls. Group them by theme.
2. **What you know better than most.** List the topics where you can say something specific, from experience, that a generic account could not.
3. **What supports the business.** Note which topics lead naturally toward what you sell, without being a sales pitch.

A topic that appears in all three lists is a strong candidate. A topic that only appears in one, such as something you enjoy but your audience never asks about, is a hobby, not a pillar.

### Pillar examples for different businesses

These are illustrative starting points, not prescriptions:

- **Independent bookshop:** staff picks, author events, "if you liked this, try that", behind the counter.
- **Freelance accountant:** tax-season reminders, plain-English explainers, client questions answered, small-business money habits.
- **B2B software company:** product tips, customer workflows, industry commentary, team and culture.
- **Fitness coach:** technique demos, client progress (with permission), myths explained, weekly routines.

Notice how each pillar is a subject you can discuss in many formats. None of them depends on a single video style or trend.

## Test each pillar before committing

Run two quick tests on every candidate pillar.

**The 12-idea test.** Set a timer for ten minutes and list post ideas under the pillar. If you reach 12, it has room to grow. If you stall at four, merge it into another pillar or widen it.

**The repeatability test.** Ask whether you can create a post on this pillar in a normal week, with the people and tools you already have. A pillar that needs an interview with a busy expert every time will collapse the moment that person is unavailable.

Drop any pillar that fails both. Having three strong pillars is better than five thin ones.

## Plan around your pillars

Once the pillars are set, planning becomes a matching exercise. Here is a simple way to run it.

1. **Assign a weight to each pillar.** For example, if you post three times a week, you might give your main pillar two slots and rotate the others through the third. Adjust based on what your audience responds to.
2. **Give each pillar a recurring slot.** Some teams attach pillars to days, such as customer questions on Tuesday. Others rotate freely. Either works, as long as every pillar appears regularly.
3. **Keep an idea bank per pillar.** When a thought arrives, drop it into the right pillar list. On planning day you choose from the bank rather than starting from a blank page.
4. **Tag every post with its pillar.** Without tags you can't tell later which themes are working.

### Pair pillars with campaigns

Pillars are always-on. Campaigns are time-limited pushes, such as a product launch or a seasonal sale. A campaign should borrow from your pillars, not replace them. During a launch week, you might create launch posts that still fit your "customer questions" and "behind the scenes" pillars, so your feed does not suddenly feel like a different account.

## Review which pillars earn their place

After a month or two, compare performance by pillar. Look at the metrics tied to your goal: replies and saves for conversation, link clicks for traffic, reach for awareness. Keep the comparison fair by looking at several posts per pillar, since a single post can be a fluke.

Then make one change at a time. Increase the weight of the strongest pillar, rework the weakest one by changing its angle, or retire it. Resist the urge to rebuild everything after one slow week. Pillars are meant to be stable enough that the audience learns them.

## Common pitfalls

- **Too many pillars.** Seven or eight themes read as "no theme" to your audience.
- **Pillars that are really products.** "Our new feature" is an announcement, not a pillar.
- **Ignoring platform differences.** A pillar can stay the same while the post changes. As of 2026, a LinkedIn post can run to 3,000 characters while a Threads post is limited to 500, so the same idea needs different treatment.
- **Never refreshing.** Review the pillars every quarter against your goals and customer questions.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many content pillars should I have?

Three to five is a practical range for most brands. Fewer than three can feel repetitive, and more than five usually dilutes your message and stretches your production time.

### What is the difference between content pillars and content categories?

The terms overlap, but pillars are chosen strategically to serve a goal and an audience need. Categories are often just labels for what you already post. Pillars drive what you create next, while categories describe what exists.

### Do content pillars work for personal brands?

Yes. A personal brand can use pillars such as your expertise, your process, lessons learned and your opinions on industry news. The same tests apply: each pillar should be repeatable and relevant to your audience.

### How often should I change my pillars?

Review them quarterly and change them only when the evidence supports it, such as a pillar consistently producing little engagement or your business goal shifting. Frequent changes stop your audience from learning what you stand for.
