A social media report that clients actually read is short, starts with a plain-language summary, ties every metric to a goal they agreed to, explains why results moved, and ends with next steps. Aim for a few pages with headline numbers up front and detail in an appendix for anyone who wants it.

## Key takeaways

- A social media report should answer three questions: what happened, why it happened and what happens next.
- Open with a short written summary, because many clients read only the first page.
- Report only the metrics linked to the client's goals, and keep the rest in an appendix.
- Add one or two sentences of interpretation next to every chart, since numbers without explanation invite the wrong conclusions.
- Use the same structure every month so clients learn where to look and trends become obvious.

## Start with the reader, not the data

Most reports fail because they are written for the person who produced them. A report built around what the platform exports tends to be long, dense and full of numbers the reader cannot judge as good or bad.

Before you build anything, find out three things from the client: their top one or two business goals, who will read the report, and how they prefer to receive it. A founder who wants to know whether social is worth the spend needs a different report from a marketing manager who wants to refine tactics. If you cannot get clear answers, send a draft and ask what was useful and what was skipped.

## The structure of a social media report that gets read

A reliable structure puts decisions first and details last. This monthly social media report layout works for most small brands and agencies.

1. **Executive summary (3 to 5 sentences).** The headline result, the most important change since last period and the main recommendation.
2. **Goal scorecard.** Three to five metrics tied to agreed goals, each with its value, the previous period and the change.
3. **Highlights.** The top posts of the month, with a line on why each worked.
4. **Lowlights and learnings.** One or two posts or experiments that underperformed, and what you learned from them. Including these builds trust.
5. **Channel breakdown.** A small table comparing platforms on the same metrics.
6. **Next month's plan.** Two to four specific actions, including any tests.
7. **Appendix.** Detailed charts, posting calendar and definitions.

Keep the first six items to a few pages. Anything extra goes at the back, where it supports questions without crowding the story.

A summary might read like this (illustrative): "Reach grew compared with last month, mainly because of three short videos. Link clicks held steady, and two sign-ups came from tagged links. Next month we will publish more video and test a stronger call to action." Three sentences cover result, cause and plan, and a busy reader can stop there and still be informed.

## Pick metrics that match the client's goals

Different goals call for different headline numbers. Match them deliberately, and drop anything that does not serve the goal.

| Client goal | Headline metrics | Supporting metrics |
|---|---|---|
| Awareness | Reach, follower growth | Impressions, share of audience that is new |
| Engagement and community | Engagement rate, saves and shares | Comments, response time |
| Traffic | Link clicks, sessions from tagged links | Click-through by platform |
| Leads or sales | Conversions from social, cost per lead | Assisted conversions, promo-code uses |

Define each metric once, in plain language, in the appendix or a footnote. If you report engagement rate, say whether it is calculated by reach, impressions or followers, and keep that definition fixed. Changing it between reports makes trends impossible to trust.

Beware of reporting only vanity numbers. A report that celebrates total followers and likes but says nothing about traffic or enquiries often leads to awkward questions at renewal time. If a result did not move, say so and explain your plan.

## Write the story around each chart

Charts need captions that do the thinking. For each chart or table, add a sentence for what happened, a sentence for why you think it happened, and where relevant a sentence for what you will do about it.

For example: "Reach rose compared with last month, driven mainly by two short videos. We plan to publish more of this format next month and compare saves against our carousels." Notice the structure: result, cause, action. It uses plain language and avoids jargon such as "impressions velocity" or "algorithmic lift".

When results fell, resist the urge to hide it in a footnote. State the drop, give the most likely reasons (such as fewer posts, a holiday period or a format change) and say what you will test. Clients tend to forgive a dip they were told about and tend to distrust one they discovered themselves.

## Design and delivery choices

Good presentation makes a report easier to scan, which is half of getting it read.

- **Use the client's context.** Add their logo, brand colours and name, or your agency's if it is a white-label deliverable.
- **One idea per chart.** Avoid crowding several metrics into one visual.
- **Show change, not just totals.** Add the comparison with the previous period next to each number.
- **Send it on a predictable schedule.** For example, the first working week of each month, so clients expect it.
- **Offer a live or shareable link in addition to a PDF**, so stakeholders can look at the numbers without searching their inbox.
- **Offer a quick call for the first few months.** Walk through the first page together to learn which parts the client values.

Review the report before sending. Check dates, platform names, client names and any numbers that look unusual. A single wrong figure can undermine confidence in everything else.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a social media report be?

A few pages for the main story, plus an appendix for details, is enough for most clients. Length matters less than whether the first page tells the reader what happened and what comes next.

### How often should I send client reports?

Monthly is the most common rhythm, with a quarterly review that looks at longer trends. Send brief weekly updates only when a campaign or launch needs close tracking.

### Which metrics should I include in a client report?

Include the few metrics linked to the client's agreed goals, plus the previous-period comparison. Keep supporting detail in an appendix instead of the main body.

### Should I include bad results in a report?

Yes. Report underperforming posts and falling metrics alongside your explanation and plan. Clients usually trust a report more when it includes honest context.
