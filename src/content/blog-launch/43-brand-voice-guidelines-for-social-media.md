Brand voice is the consistent personality in how a brand writes, shown through word choice, sentence rhythm and attitude. Brand voice guidelines for social media are a short document, ideally one page, that lists your core traits, words to use and avoid, platform tone shifts and example posts so anyone can write like you.

## Key takeaways

- Voice stays the same everywhere, while tone flexes by platform and situation, so guidelines should define both.
- A useful voice guide is short and testable: three or four traits, each with a "we are / we are not" pair and a real example.
- Word lists, emoji habits and call-to-action style are the quickest rules to apply, and they also give AI tools something concrete to follow.
- Build the guide from your own best posts, then review it every few months against what you actually publish.

## Voice versus tone: a quick distinction

Voice is who you are; tone is how you sound in a given moment. A friendly neighbourhood bakery might always have a warm, plain-spoken voice. Its tone is playful in a Saturday-special post and calm and direct when announcing a closure.

This matters because guidelines that only describe adjectives ("friendly, professional, innovative") fail in practice. Nearly every brand says those three words, and they do not tell a new writer what to do on a Tuesday. Good guidelines translate each trait into behaviour: sentence length, vocabulary, punctuation, how you handle humour, how you respond to criticism.

## How to build your brand voice in five steps

You can finish a first version in an afternoon. The aim is a working draft you improve, not a perfect manifesto.

1. **Collect ten samples.** Gather your best-performing and most "on-brand" posts, emails, website lines and customer replies. Include a few that felt off, too, as contrast.
2. **Mark what you notice.** Highlight repeated words, typical sentence length, how you open and close, emoji use and punctuation. Patterns you see in samples are your real voice.
3. **Choose three or four traits.** Pick traits that are specific and opposable. "Direct" works because its opposite (hedging) is a real option. "Quality" does not, because nobody aims for the opposite.
4. **Write a "we are / we are not" line for each trait.** For example: "Direct: we state the point in the first sentence. We are not blunt to the point of rude."
5. **Add a proof example.** For each trait, include one real sentence from your past posts that shows it.

If you work with several people, ask each to rewrite the same sample post using the draft guide. Compare the results. Where they diverge, the guide is too vague.

## A copy-and-paste brand voice template

Use this structure as a one-page document. Fill in each field with your own details.

**1. Brand in one sentence.** What you do and for whom, in plain words.

**2. Voice traits (3-4).** For each: trait name, "we are / we are not", one real example sentence.

**3. Vocabulary.**
- Words and phrases we like (for example, product names, nicknames for customers, preferred spellings such as UK or US English).
- Words we avoid (buzzwords, slang that does not fit, competitor comparisons, claims we cannot back up).

**4. Style mechanics.**
- Sentence length and paragraph habits.
- Emoji: how many, which ones, where.
- Exclamation marks, capitalisation and punctuation rules.
- How we write hashtags and mentions.

**5. Calls to action.** The two or three ways we ask for a click, comment or purchase, and the ones we never use.

**6. Platform tone shifts.** One line per platform (see below).

**7. Sensitive situations.** How we respond to complaints, mistakes, news events and political topics, and who approves those posts.

**8. Before and after.** One generic sentence rewritten in our voice.

## Adapt tone for each platform

Keep the voice stable and change the tone. Describe each platform in one line so writers know how far to flex.

- **LinkedIn:** more structured and considered, with room for a short story or lesson. Posts can run up to 3,000 characters as of 2026, but the first two lines still have to earn the click.
- **Instagram:** warmer and more visual, with captions that support the image or video. The caption limit is 2,200 characters.
- **X and Bluesky:** compact and immediate. X allows 280 characters on a standard account, and Bluesky allows 300 graphemes.
- **Threads:** conversational and informal, within a 500-character limit.
- **TikTok and YouTube Shorts:** spoken, fast and personality-led. Captions support the video.

These are directions for tone, not rules for what to say. If your audience on one platform behaves differently from the usual pattern, trust your own results.

## Put the guidelines to work

A guide nobody opens is decoration. Make it part of daily workflow so it shapes what gets published.

### Use it as an editing checklist

Turn your word lists and style mechanics into a five-item check that an editor runs before approving a post. If the post fails two items, send it back. This also keeps feedback objective, because the comment is "we avoid that phrase," not "I don't like it."

### Feed it to your AI tools

If you use AI for drafts, paste your traits, preferred words and banned words into the tool's settings or prompt every time. The model has no way to guess your voice, and concrete rules (such as "no exclamation marks" or "use 'customers', never 'users'") shape output far more than adjectives do. Always read the result against the guide, because a model can still drift.

### Review it quarterly

Voices evolve as brands do. Every few months, compare your guide against your latest ten posts. If you keep breaking a rule on purpose, the rule probably needs updating. If new team members misread a rule, rewrite it with a clearer example.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Writing a guide that fits any company.** If you could swap in a competitor's name without changing a word, it is too generic.
- **Making it too long.** Past one or two pages, people stop reading. Link to deeper material instead.
- **Forgetting the "not."** Defining what you are not does as much work as defining what you are.
- **Skipping examples.** A rule without a sample sentence is easy to misinterpret.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between brand voice and tone?

Brand voice is the stable personality of your writing across every channel. Tone is how that voice adjusts to a platform, audience or situation, such as being lighter on Instagram and more measured when handling a complaint.

### How long should a voice guide be?

One page is enough for most social media teams. Include traits, word lists, style mechanics, platform tone notes and a few examples, and link out to longer material only if people need it.

### Does a small business or solo creator need written guidelines?

Yes, though a short version is enough. Even a half-page note listing three traits, a few favourite and banned words and your emoji habits will keep your posts consistent and speed up any drafting help you get later.

### How do I teach an AI tool my brand voice?

Give it concrete rules and real samples: preferred words, words to avoid, emoji and call-to-action style, and a couple of your best posts. Then edit every output, since the tool follows instructions but does not know your brand on its own.
