A good client approval social media process has four parts: a named approver, a fixed review window, a single place to review posts, and a rule for what happens if nobody responds. Agree on those four things in writing before the first post goes out, and approvals stop being the thing that delays publishing.

## Key takeaways

- Client approval social media delays have predictable causes: unclear approvers, scattered feedback channels, and no consequence for silence. Fix those three before anything else.
- Limit each approval to one named decision-maker and, at most, one backup, because group reviews multiply revision rounds.
- Ask clients to categorize change requests (wording, brand voice, visuals, timing, compliance) so feedback is specific and repeatable.
- Write the "no response" rule into the contract or onboarding document, and apply it consistently.

## Why client approvals stall

Client approval social media workflows rarely stall because clients dislike your work. They stall because reviewing social posts is a small task that sits at the bottom of a busy person's day, and your process makes it harder than it needs to be.

Look at how feedback usually arrives. A screenshot in WhatsApp. A reply on an email thread from last Tuesday. A comment on a shared document. A phone call where the owner says "make it pop more". Each channel needs you to translate, collect, and reconcile, and each round of vague feedback invites another round.

Three structural problems cause most delays:

- **Nobody owns the decision.** If three people at the client can approve, each assumes another will.
- **Feedback is unstructured.** "Not feeling it" gives you nothing to fix.
- **Silence has no consequence.** If a late response costs nothing, deadlines become suggestions.

## Build a client approval social media process in five steps

The steps below work whether you use a spreadsheet or a dedicated tool. Adapt the numbers to your client's pace.

1. **Name the approver and a backup.** One person decides. A second person steps in if the first is unavailable. Everyone else can comment, but only the approver's decision counts.
2. **Set the review window.** Pick a window you can staff, for example 48 hours (an illustrative figure), and state it in the onboarding document.
3. **Define the submission rhythm.** Send posts in a weekly or fortnightly batch rather than one at a time, so the client reviews once, not daily.
4. **Limit revision rounds.** Include a set number of rounds, such as two, in your scope. Extra rounds can be billed or deferred.
5. **Write the silence rule.** Decide what happens if the window passes: the post is held, or it publishes as written, or it is replaced with a pre-approved evergreen post. Hold is the safest default for regulated or sensitive clients.

### Choose the silence rule deliberately

The silence rule is the most important and the most uncomfortable. "Publishes automatically after 48 hours" is convenient for you but risky if the client later claims they never saw it. "Holds until approved" protects the client but can leave gaps in the calendar.

A middle path is to keep a small bank of pre-approved evergreen posts (generic tips, FAQs, behind-the-scenes images) that fill gaps when approval is late. The client has already said yes to those, so the calendar stays consistent without overriding anyone.

## Make feedback specific with change categories

Structured feedback is faster to act on than free text. Instead of an open comment box, ask reviewers to pick a category first, then add notes. A workable set of categories is:

- **Copy and typos:** grammar, spelling, wording
- **Brand voice:** tone is off or a banned term was used
- **Media and visuals:** image quality, aspect ratio, wrong picture
- **Legal and compliance:** disclosures, claims, copyright, regulatory limits
- **Timing and strategy:** date, channel, or campaign changes

Categories help you in two ways. First, you can route the fix: a wording issue goes to the copywriter, a visual issue goes to the designer. Second, you can spot patterns. If most of a client's change requests are "brand voice", your intake brief probably missed something, and tightening it will cut future rounds.

## Message templates you can copy

Templates keep your tone consistent and save time. Adjust the details, and keep them short.

### Submission message

> Hi [Name], the posts for [dates] are ready for your review. Please approve or request changes by [day, time]. If we have not heard back by then, we will hold the posts and use a pre-approved evergreen post instead. Thanks!

### Reminder message

> A quick reminder that [number] posts are waiting for your review and the window closes [day, time]. If it is easier, tell us which ones are fine and we will start publishing those.

The second sentence matters. Partial approval keeps the calendar moving instead of blocking everything on one disputed caption.

### Change request guide for clients

> When you request changes, please tell us what kind of change it is (wording, brand voice, visuals, timing, or compliance) and, if you can, what you would like instead. "Make it shorter" or "use the other photo" is enough.

## Give clients one place to review

Where approval happens matters as much as how. A client who must log in to a complicated tool will avoid it, and a client who can open a clean review page may actually use it.

A client portal is a restricted, client-facing view where reviewers can see scheduled content, approve it, or send it back, without access to the rest of your workspace. Whichever tool you use, check that the view shows the post as it will appear on each platform, because clients often object to previews they did not understand, such as a shortened caption or a cropped image.

Give clients the narrowest role that lets them do their job, usually approving content and viewing their own analytics. Internal comments and other clients' work stay behind your own permissions.

## Handle the hard cases

Even a good client approval social media process hits awkward moments. Here are three, with a suggested response.

**The client rewrites everything.** Point back to the brief and the agreed content pillars, and ask which goal the rewrite serves. If the direction has genuinely changed, update the brief.

**The approver goes silent for a week.** Follow the silence rule you wrote down. Apply it kindly but consistently, and mention it in the next call, not as a reprimand but as a scheduling reminder.

**A late-stage compliance concern.** Treat these as a stop, not a negotiation. Hold the post, ask the client to confirm with whoever handles legal or regulatory matters, and record the decision. You are not the final authority on regulations in the client's industry or country.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should clients have to approve social media posts?

There is no standard figure, because the right length depends on your lead time and the client's pace. A window of one to three working days is a reasonable starting point, as long as it leaves you time to make changes before the post goes live.

### What should happen if a client does not respond?

Decide in advance and put it in writing. Common options are holding the post, substituting a pre-approved evergreen post, or publishing as written. Holding is the safest option for sensitive or regulated clients.

### Should clients approve every single post?

Not necessarily. Some clients approve a monthly plan and then only review high-stakes posts, such as promotions, announcements, or anything with claims. Start with full review and relax it as trust builds.

### How many revision rounds should be included?

Two rounds is a reasonable starting point, but it is a business decision, not a rule. State the number in your scope so extra rounds are a conscious choice rather than a silent cost.
