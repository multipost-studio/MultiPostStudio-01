Social media team roles usually split into five jobs: strategy, content creation, publishing, community management, and analytics. In a small team one person covers several jobs, so the key is to separate permissions, not people: let creators draft, let a second person approve or publish, and give everyone only the access their work requires.

## Key takeaways

- Social media team roles describe jobs (what someone is responsible for), while a permission describes access (what the tool lets them do). Define both, and do not assume they are the same.
- Separate drafting from approving so no one publishes their own unreviewed work, even in a team of three.
- Apply least privilege: give each person the narrowest access that lets them do the job, and widen it only when there is a reason.
- Clients, freelancers and executives usually need narrow roles, such as approve-only or view-only.
- Review access every quarter and remove it promptly when someone leaves or a project ends.

## Roles versus permissions

A role is a set of responsibilities, and a permission is a specific action the software allows. Mixing them up is the root of most access problems.

Consider a job title like "Social Media Manager". It might involve strategy, writing, replying to comments, and reporting. That is a role. In the tool, you decide which permissions support it: can they connect new channels, delete posts, approve content, or invite members? Two people with the same title can reasonably have different permissions, depending on seniority and the clients they handle.

Start with the social media team roles, then translate them into access. Doing it the other way around, by handing out whatever level of access feels convenient, leads to either an over-privileged team or a bottleneck.

## The five social media team roles

Every social media operation, whether one person or twenty, covers the same five jobs. Seeing them separately helps you assign them.

### Strategy and planning

The strategist sets goals, picks content pillars, chooses channels, and owns the calendar. They need to see everything and configure how work flows, including approval steps, but they do not necessarily need to publish.

### Content creation

Writers, designers, and video editors draft captions and build assets. They need to create and edit content and manage media, and they rarely need to connect channels or change billing.

### Review and publishing

Someone with editorial judgement checks drafts for accuracy, tone, and compliance, then schedules or publishes them. In smaller teams this is the account lead. In regulated industries it may include legal or compliance sign-off.

### Community management

Community managers reply to comments and messages, escalate complaints, and feed audience insight back to the strategist. They need inbox access, and often the ability to assign conversations to colleagues.

### Analytics and reporting

Analysts or account managers track results and produce reports. They need to see metrics and build reports, but not edit live content.

## A simple permission matrix

The matrix below shows one reasonable starting point. Treat it as an example to adapt, not a rule.

| Role | Create and edit drafts | Approve | Publish | Reply in inbox | View analytics | Manage team and channels |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Creator | Yes | No | No | Yes | Yes | No |
| Editor | Yes | No | Yes | Yes | Yes | No |
| Manager | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Analyst | No | No | No | No | Yes | No |
| Viewer | No | No | No | No | Yes | No |
| Client | No | Yes | No | No | Yes | No |

The pattern is intentional. Access to *make* content, *approve* content, and *change the system* sits with different people, and the further a role is from the daily work, the narrower its access.

If your tool allows custom roles, create them for edge cases rather than stretching a standard role. A freelance designer who only needs to upload media is a better candidate for a custom role than for a full editor seat.

## Four rules for assigning access

These rules apply whichever tool you use.

1. **Never let people approve their own work.** Even in a team of two, have the other person review. Self-approval is how typos and wrong links reach live accounts.
2. **Use least privilege.** Start narrow and widen on request. It is easy to grant more access and awkward to take it back.
3. **Keep channel connection and billing with a few people.** Connecting accounts and changing billing have lasting consequences, so limit them to an owner and one backup.
4. **Give outside people narrow roles.** Clients, freelancers, and executives can add comments and approvals without seeing internal drafts or other clients' work.

### Handle the "wearing many hats" problem

Small teams cannot always split every role. When one person drafts, reviews, and publishes, add a lightweight check instead: schedule posts at least a few hours ahead, then reread them with fresh eyes, or swap reviews with a peer in another team. Where no peer exists, a pre-publish checklist (links work, previews look right, tags are correct, alt text added) catches most avoidable errors.

## Document responsibilities, not only access

Permissions say what people can do, but a team also needs to know what they are expected to do. A short responsibilities document answers questions before they become arguments.

For each role, write down:

- What decisions this person makes alone
- What they must escalate, and to whom
- How quickly they respond to comments, approvals, or requests
- Who covers when they are away

The "who covers" line is the one most teams skip. Holidays, illness, and resignations are certain, and an account with no backup is a single point of failure. Ensure at least two people can publish for each account, and that more than one person knows how each channel is connected.

For incidents, add an escalation path. If a post causes a backlash or a platform issue arises, who decides whether to pause scheduled content? Name that person ahead of time.

## Review access on a schedule

Access drifts. Freelancers finish projects, interns graduate, and people move teams, yet their accounts remain active. Once a quarter, list everyone with access to each workspace and ask whether they still need it, at that level.

Pay particular attention to former team members, agencies you no longer work with, and anyone who has publishing power but has not used it. Removing unused access reduces risk, and an audit log that records who changed what makes the review and any investigation far easier.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which social media team roles do you need?

Most teams cover five jobs: strategy, content creation, review and publishing, community management, and analytics. A small team can combine them, but should still separate drafting from approving.

### What permissions should a social media intern or freelancer have?

Usually the ability to create drafts and upload media, but not to publish, delete, connect channels, or manage billing. Add more access only when the work requires it, and remove it when the project ends.

### Who should have admin access to social media accounts?

Limit full administrative access to a small number of trusted people, typically an owner and one backup, so that someone can always recover access without sharing logins widely.

### How often should you review team access?

At least quarterly, and immediately when someone leaves or a contract ends. Include clients and agencies you work with, not only employees.
