A strong client onboarding social media process is a checklist with five parts: a signed scope, an intake questionnaire, safe access to the client's accounts, an agreed approval process, and a first-30-days plan. Completing all five before posting begins prevents most of the rework, delays and misunderstandings that damage new agency relationships.

## Key takeaways

- The first two weeks of a client relationship decide how smooth the rest will be, so treat client onboarding social media work as a defined project with an owner and a deadline.
- Collect goals, audience, tone, restrictions and approval contacts in a written intake questionnaire rather than relying on a single call.
- Request access through each platform's own permission tools instead of asking for passwords, and keep a record of what access you were given.
- Agree on the approval process, response times and reporting cadence before creating any content.
- Launch with a short, realistic first-30-days plan that includes a review checkpoint.

## Prepare before the kickoff call

Preparation turns a kickoff call from an interview into a decision meeting. Do three things in advance.

1. **Confirm the scope in writing.** List the channels, posting frequency, content types, community management hours, reporting, and revision rounds that are included. Anything not listed is out of scope until agreed.
2. **Send the intake questionnaire.** Ask the client to complete it before the call, so you spend the call discussing answers rather than collecting them.
3. **Review their current presence.** Look at each existing profile, recent posts, and competitors. Note anything unclear, such as inconsistent handles, outdated bios, or abandoned accounts, and bring those questions to the call.

Also assign an internal owner. One account manager should be responsible for completing the checklist, and a backup should know where everything is.

## The intake questionnaire

An intake questionnaire gathers the information you need to create content that sounds like the client and serves their business. Group questions into themes so the form feels manageable.

### Business and goals

- What does the business sell, and who buys it?
- What do you want social media to achieve in the next three to six months (awareness, leads, bookings, community, hiring)?
- How will you judge success, and which numbers matter most to you?
- Are there upcoming launches, events, seasons, or campaigns we should plan around?

### Audience and competitors

- Who is your ideal customer, and what questions do they ask most often?
- Which competitors or accounts do you admire, and which do you want to avoid resembling?
- Where does your audience spend time, and which channels have you tried?

### Brand voice and visuals

- Describe your tone in three words, and give two examples of posts you liked.
- Are there words, phrases, or topics we must never use?
- Where are your logo files, color codes, fonts, and photography?
- Do you have a brand guidelines document?

### Operations and restrictions

- Who approves content, who is the backup, and how quickly can they respond?
- Does anything need legal, compliance, or leadership sign-off?
- Do you have industry rules or claims restrictions we should know about?
- Who should handle comments, complaints, and direct messages, and what should be escalated?

Keep the questionnaire short enough that people actually finish it. If a question will not change what you do, remove it.

## Get access safely

Access is the most delicate part of onboarding, because it involves the client's public identity. Handle it carefully, and avoid asking for passwords.

Ask the client to grant access through the platform's own permission system, such as adding you as an admin or partner through their business tools, or authorizing your publishing software directly through the platform's official connection flow. This keeps access revocable, avoids sharing credentials by chat or email, and prevents two-step verification codes from becoming a daily obstacle.

Use this access checklist:

- [ ] List every profile in scope, with its exact handle and URL
- [ ] Confirm who legally owns each account and who the admin is
- [ ] Obtain access through official permission tools, not shared passwords
- [ ] Connect each channel to the client's own workspace in your scheduling tool
- [ ] Test by scheduling a harmless draft, then deleting it
- [ ] Record what access you hold and when it should be reviewed

Also confirm that the client keeps ownership. If the relationship ends, they should still control their accounts, and your removal should take minutes.

## Agree the working agreement

The working agreement is a one-page document stating how you will work together. It prevents the common friction points before they arise.

Include:

- **Approval process:** the named approver, backup, review window, and what happens if no response arrives.
- **Communication:** the main channel for day-to-day messages, who is on it, and expected response times on both sides.
- **Reporting:** how often reports arrive, what they contain, and when the review call happens.
- **Revisions and extras:** how many revision rounds are included, and how out-of-scope requests are handled.
- **Crisis contact:** who to call if a post causes a problem or an urgent issue appears outside working hours.

Walk through it on the kickoff call rather than just emailing it. Questions raised then are far cheaper than disagreements raised three months later.

## Plan the first 30 days of client onboarding social media

A structured start builds confidence and gives you data early. Here is one outline, labelled as an example.

1. **Days 1 to 5: foundations.** Complete the intake, collect access, audit the current profiles, and fix obvious basics such as bios, links, profile images, and handles.
2. **Days 6 to 10: strategy.** Define three to five content pillars, choose channels, set posting frequency, and draft a content calendar for the first month.
3. **Days 11 to 18: production and approval.** Create the first batch, send it for approval using the agreed process, and note the types of feedback you receive.
4. **Days 19 to 25: launch.** Schedule approved posts, set up your reply routine, and start tracking results against the goals from intake.
5. **Days 26 to 30: review.** Hold a short check-in call. Share early results, ask what is working from the client's side, and adjust the plan.

Do not promise results in this window. Early data is thin, and the first month mainly proves that the process works. Frame the review as calibration, not judgement.

## Common onboarding mistakes

- **Posting before approvals are agreed.** The first disagreement then becomes a process argument.
- **Accepting vague goals.** "More engagement" is not a goal. Ask what it would change for the business.
- **Collecting information but never using it.** Turn the questionnaire into a one-page summary your whole team can reference.
- **Treating onboarding as a one-off.** Revisit the intake answers at the 90-day mark, since goals change.

## Frequently asked questions

### What should a client onboarding social media checklist include?

It should cover a written scope, an intake questionnaire, access to accounts through official permission tools, brand assets, an approval process, a reporting cadence, and a first-30-days plan.

### How long does social media client onboarding take?

It varies with the client's responsiveness and the number of channels. Plan for a few weeks, but speed matters less than having access, approvals, and brand rules settled first.

### Should clients give you their social media passwords?

Avoid it. Ask for access through each platform's own role-based tools or an official connection flow, which can be revoked without changing the client's login.

### What questions should an intake questionnaire ask?

Ask about business goals, audience, competitors, tone of voice, restricted topics, brand assets, approval contacts, and who handles comments and complaints. Keep it short enough to finish in one sitting.
