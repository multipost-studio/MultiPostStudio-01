To work out how to price social media management, define the scope in deliverables, estimate the hours each one takes, multiply by the hourly rate you need to earn, add a buffer for revisions, then package the result as a retainer, project fee, or per-channel price. Price from your own time and value, not from guessing what competitors charge.

## Key takeaways

- Knowing how to price social media management starts with scope: a number means nothing until you have listed channels, posts per week, community management, meetings, reporting, and revision rounds.
- Estimate hours per deliverable, apply the rate you need to earn, and add a buffer, because unpaid revisions and calls are the most common cause of underpricing.
- Retainers suit ongoing work, project fees suit launches and audits, and per-channel pricing is easy to explain but can hide uneven workloads.
- Every figure in this article is an illustrative example to show the method. Your rates depend on your costs, skills, market, and clients.
- Put the scope and exclusions in writing, and review prices at least once a year.

## Start with scope, not the number

How to price social media management begins with scope, because a price only makes sense relative to what the client gets. Before any arithmetic, write the scope as a list of deliverables with quantities. Vague scopes like "manage our social media" are how a fair price becomes an unprofitable one.

Cover these items:

- **Channels:** which platforms (for example Instagram, LinkedIn, Facebook, TikTok, Google Business Profile).
- **Volume:** posts per week per channel, plus stories, reels, or short videos if included.
- **Creation:** who writes captions, who designs graphics, and who shoots or edits video.
- **Community management:** whether you reply to comments and messages, how often, and in what hours.
- **Strategy and meetings:** planning sessions, monthly calls, and ad hoc requests.
- **Reporting:** frequency and format.
- **Revisions:** how many rounds are included.
- **Exclusions:** paid advertising management, influencer outreach, crisis response, and photo shoots are often separate services.

## Estimate your hours

Hour estimates are the foundation of a defensible price, even if you never bill hourly. Break the work into tasks and estimate each one honestly.

Here is a worked example, clearly illustrative. Suppose a client wants three channels with three posts per week each, plus light community management and a monthly report. Over a four-week month, that is 36 posts.

| Task | Estimate (hours per month) |
|---|---|
| Strategy and calendar planning | 3 |
| Creating 36 posts (about 45 minutes each) | 27 |
| Scheduling and quality checks | 4 |
| Community management (2 hours per week) | 8 |
| Reporting | 3 |
| Meetings and client communication | 2 |
| **Total** | **47** |

Add a buffer for revisions, delays, and the things nobody planned. A 15 percent buffer is used here only as an example, which brings the total to roughly 54 hours.

Posting itself is quick. Planning, creating, replying, and reporting are where the hours go.

## How to price social media management from your hours

To convert hours into a price, multiply by the rate you need to earn. That rate should cover your salary or income goal, taxes, software, equipment, unpaid admin, sales time, and a profit margin. It is usually higher than what you would pay an employee for the same hours, because you carry costs an employee does not.

Continuing the example, suppose your target rate is 50 per hour in your currency (an illustrative figure). Fifty-four hours at 50 gives about 2,700 per month. That is a floor to test against the market, not an automatic quote.

Two checks help:

1. **Divide the price by the hours you really spent last month.** If the effective rate falls well below your target, the scope or the price needs to change.
2. **Compare with the client's value.** A local business that gets bookings from social media may see value far above your hourly cost, and you can price accordingly. A client with no clear goal may not.

## Choose a pricing model

Each model trades simplicity against fairness and risk. Most agencies end up with a hybrid.

### Monthly retainer

A fixed monthly fee for a defined scope. It gives you predictable income and the client a predictable cost. The risk is scope creep, so state exclusions and an extra-work rate. Retainers suit ongoing management.

### Project fee

A one-time price for a bounded piece of work, such as a profile audit, a strategy document, a launch campaign, or a content batch. Clear start and end dates keep projects profitable. Retainers often begin after a successful project.

### Per-channel pricing

A fixed price per platform, such as 900 per channel in our example (2,700 divided by three). It is easy to explain and lets clients add or drop channels. The weakness is that channels differ: a daily short-video channel takes far more effort than a weekly LinkedIn post, so adjust by volume and content type.

### Hourly billing

You bill tracked hours. It is low-risk for you, but clients may dislike unpredictable bills. It works best for ad hoc requests and add-ons.

### Tiered packages

Two or three named packages, such as Starter, Growth, and Full Service, each with a fixed scope. Packages simplify sales conversations and let clients self-select. Build each tier from the same hour-based method so none is accidentally unprofitable.

## Protect your margin

Pricing is only half the job. The other half is making sure the scope you priced is the scope you deliver.

- **Write the scope into a contract** with deliverables, revision limits, response times, and exclusions.
- **Track time for the first two or three months**, even on a retainer, to check your estimates.
- **Define a rate for extras** and mention it when a request falls outside scope, politely and early.
- **Set a review date.** Raise prices when scope grows or costs rise, and tell clients in advance.
- **Watch revision rounds.** If a client regularly exceeds them, your brief or approval process needs attention.

Efficiency also affects margin. Reusable templates, batching, scheduling tools, and clear approval processes cut the hours per client, so the same price becomes more profitable over time.

## Present the price with confidence

Lead with outcomes and scope, then the number. Offer two or three options so the conversation becomes "which one" rather than "whether".

Avoid apologetic discounts. If a client cannot afford your scope, reduce the scope instead of the rate, for example by trimming channels or posting frequency.

Do not promise reach or follower growth. Tie your commitments to what you control: deliverables, deadlines, and reporting.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I charge hourly or a monthly retainer for social media management?

Retainers tend to suit ongoing work because income is predictable and clients know their cost. Use hourly rates for extras and one-off tasks, and base the retainer on hour estimates so it stays profitable.

### How to price social media management if I am just starting out?

Work out your hourly rate first: add up your target income, taxes, software, equipment, and unpaid time, divide by the billable hours you can realistically sell, and add a margin. Then estimate hours for a small, clearly defined scope. Your rate will depend on your location, experience, and clients.

### Is it better to price per platform or per package?

Per-platform pricing is simple but ignores workload differences between channels. Packages give clients clear choices. Whichever you pick, derive it from estimated hours for the content and tasks involved.

### Should ad management be included in the price?

Usually not. Paid advertising involves different skills, tools, and responsibilities, so it is often quoted separately, either as a flat fee or alongside the ad budget.
