Social media compliance means making sure what you publish follows the law that applies to you, each platform's rules, and your own brand policies. In practice that covers clear disclosure of paid or gifted content, honest claims, permission for images and music, and records of approvals. This article is a checklist, not legal advice.

## Key takeaways

- Social media compliance has three layers: laws and regulations in the countries you operate in, each platform's own rules, and your organization's brand and industry policies.
- Disclosure rules for sponsored, gifted or affiliate content vary by country, so check the rules where you and your audience are, and ask a qualified professional when the stakes are high.
- Platforms have their own branded-content or paid-promotion tools, and using them is usually in addition to, not instead of, clear wording in the post.
- Build compliance into the workflow with checklists, an approval step for sensitive content, and records of who approved what.
- When in doubt, hold the post and ask. A delayed post costs less than a takedown, a complaint, or a damaged relationship.

## The three layers of compliance

Compliance is easier to manage when you separate its layers, because each has a different source and a different owner.

**The legal layer** consists of laws and regulations about advertising, endorsements, privacy, intellectual property, and, in some industries, specific rules about what you can say. It varies by country, and sometimes by region or sector. This article does not quote or summarize any specific law, and you should not treat it as legal advice.

**The platform layer** is each network's terms, community guidelines, and advertising or branded-content policies. They change, and they can differ between Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, LinkedIn, and others, so check the current versions rather than relying on memory.

**The brand layer** is your own rules: tone, banned phrases, approved claims, required legal wording, and escalation paths. Your clients or your company set these, and they are often stricter than the law.

When rules conflict, follow the strictest applicable one.

## Disclosure: the checklist for paid, gifted and affiliate content

Disclosure means telling your audience when there is a commercial relationship behind a post. The aim is that people can tell when content is an advertisement or when someone was paid or rewarded to promote something. Rules about what counts and how to word it differ by country, so treat the following as questions to ask rather than answers.

Use this checklist before publishing any partnered or promotional content:

- [ ] Is there a commercial relationship? Consider payment, free products, discounts, commission links, trips, or any benefit.
- [ ] Have we checked the disclosure rules in the countries where we operate and where the audience is?
- [ ] Is the disclosure clear, in plain language, and easy to see, rather than buried in a long block of hashtags or hidden behind a "more" cut-off?
- [ ] Is the disclosure placed where people will see it, for example at the start of the caption and, for video, spoken or shown on screen as well?
- [ ] Have we used the platform's branded-content or paid-promotion tool, where one exists?
- [ ] Are creators and partners briefed in writing on how to disclose?
- [ ] Do we have a signed agreement that covers disclosure, usage rights, and approval?
- [ ] Are we monitoring partner posts after they go live?

Platforms have their own tools. At the time of writing, for example, Instagram offers a paid partnership label, and YouTube and TikTok provide settings for declaring paid promotion or branded content. Features and names change, so check each platform's current help pages. These tools often work alongside, not instead of, your own clear wording, and legal requirements can apply even where a platform label is used.

Disclosure is not only for influencers. If employees or executives post about the company's products, give them simple guidance on making that connection clear.

## Claims, accuracy and sensitive topics

Claims are statements that something is true, such as "the fastest", "clinically proven" or "guaranteed results". They are a common source of complaints and regulatory attention.

Before publishing a claim, check:

- [ ] Can we substantiate it with evidence we hold, and is that evidence current?
- [ ] Is the wording accurate when read alone, without the surrounding context?
- [ ] Are prices, dates, and offer terms correct, including exclusions?
- [ ] Do special rules apply in our industry, such as health, finance, alcohol, gambling, or children's products?
- [ ] Have we avoided implying endorsements or partnerships that do not exist?
- [ ] Does anything require standard legal wording or a disclaimer?

For regulated industries, put a named reviewer in the approval chain and have them sign off on templates in advance. If a post needs a qualified opinion, hold it until you have it.

## Permissions, privacy and records

Posts often include things you do not own, such as photos, music, quotes, and customers' personal information. Check permissions before you publish.

### Images, music and user content

- [ ] Do we have the right to use this image, video, font, or audio, including on social media specifically?
- [ ] If we are reposting a customer's content, did we ask and keep the reply?
- [ ] Is a platform's own library of licensed music suitable for a business account, or is it for personal use only? Check the current terms.
- [ ] Have we credited the creator as agreed?

### People and privacy

- [ ] Have people shown in photos or video agreed to appear, and are minors involved?
- [ ] Does the post show customer names, order details, or other personal data?

### Records

Keep a record of approvals, permissions, and partner agreements for as long as your policies or local rules require, and ask a qualified adviser how long that is. A simple log should show the post, who approved it, when, and what evidence supported any claim. If a complaint arrives months later, this record is what you will want.

## Build social media compliance into your workflow

A checklist only works if it sits where the work happens. Aim to make the compliant path the easy one.

1. **Write a one-page policy.** Cover disclosure wording, claims rules, permissions, banned phrases, and who to ask.
2. **Add a compliance checkpoint to approvals.** Ask reviewers to flag legal or compliance concerns separately from copy and design edits, so serious issues are visible.
3. **Use templates for repeat disclosures.** Standard disclosure wording for sponsored posts reduces errors and saves time.
4. **Limit who can publish.** Restrict publishing rights on sensitive accounts, and use roles so drafts are reviewed first.
5. **Brief creators and freelancers** on your rules before they start.
6. **Review quarterly.** Platform rules and regulations change.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I have to disclose sponsored posts on social media?

Disclosure rules vary by country, and many places expect a clear label when there is a commercial relationship. Check the rules where you and your audience are located, use the platform's branded-content tools where available, and consult a qualified professional if unsure.

### Is using a platform's paid partnership label enough?

Not necessarily. Platform tools help, but legal expectations can go beyond them, and the clearest approach is usually to combine the label with plain wording in the post. Check current local rules and platform policies.

### Who is responsible for disclosure: the brand or the creator?

Responsibility can fall on both, depending on where you are and the circumstances. Brands should brief creators in writing, include disclosure terms in contracts, and monitor posts after publication.

### How often should a social media compliance checklist be updated?

Review it at least quarterly, and whenever a platform changes its rules, you enter a new market, or you start working in a regulated industry.
